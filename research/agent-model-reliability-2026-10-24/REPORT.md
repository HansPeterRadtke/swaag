## Answer 1 — numeric versus ordered textual relevance

Experimental scales were deliberately equivalent: integer 0–8 versus nine ordered constrained text enums mapping to the same 0–8 values, with the **same explicit role dimension, user request, candidate and independently specified expected ordinal answer**. Each requested output used a strict server-side JSON schema. This is an ordinal judgment, not a calibrated truth probability or percentage.

Live results from the strict exact-match oracle:

- **Coder Q4:** numeric, zero of two exact; answers 3 versus gold 8 for the user's explicit blocked-worker-status information, and 1 versus gold 0 for an irrelevant old stylesheet change. Full-response times 0.55 and 0.42 seconds. Categorical, two of two exact, both `critical` (mapped to 8) on the blocked-status fixture; times 0.52 and 0.35 seconds.
- **Qwen3.8 Q8:** numeric, one of one exact (`8`); categorical, one of one exact (`critical`); same explicit blocked-status fixture and seed 17, times 2.06 and 1.65 seconds.
- **Within-identical-case/model/seed comparisons:** Coder seed 42: numeric 3 versus categorical 8, expected 8. Qwen3.8 seed 17: both 8, expected 8. These are the **only two** properly matched encoding pairs. Other trials cannot be pooled as if they were paired.
- Earlier unscored Coder probe returned numeric zero on the irrelevant-stylesheet task in 0.525 seconds. Its system/prompt wording was different, so the later numeric one-point error cannot be assigned solely to numeric representation or seed instability.

Interpretation: **there is a concrete categorical advantage on one matched Coder item; no general winner is established.** The numerical Coder answer 1 versus strict gold 0 is a one-level calibration discrepancy, not evidence that it would actually show the wrong content to a user. The fixtures use researcher-authored preregistered expected labels rather than independently collected human ratings; intermediate-level calibration, downstream ranking, explanation quality, and user usefulness remain unmeasured. Request orders were not balanced and several attempts were censored by real production activity, so the tiny observed accuracy/latency figures are descriptive only. No standard statistical significance claim is warranted.

## Answer 2 — fused versus decomposed calls, context and quantization

One fused short factual-extraction Coder request on the **conditional restart** fixture returned `unknown`, correctly matching the original source's statement that no restart was confirmed. Its complete-answer elapsed time was 0.579 seconds. The next trial was refused before submission because Thor was busy. **No decomposed counterpart completed.** The split-call implementation is available and unit-tested: source-to-facts, then facts-only-to-answer, with a new supervisor gate between the stages and preservation of the stage-one raw output if the gate fails. The second stage intentionally cannot reread the source; this tests the real risk of extraction information loss. Whether splitting increases accuracy on these models remains **experimentally unanswered**.

No long-context position matrix or same-base-model quantization ablation was run. Coder-30B-A3B Q4 and Qwen3.8-27B Q8 differ **both in architecture/model/training and quantization**; comparing them cannot isolate a quantization effect. The measured timings are full-response latency on different sampled cases/output lengths, not first-token or typical application latency. The October twentieth one-initial-communication-model-call production rule remains intact; these research calls occur outside that live first-response path.

Published primary literature: [Decomposed Prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02406) reports benefits on the authors' complex task families but not universal superiority. [Lost in the Middle](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/) measures degraded use of information in middle positions of long contexts, including long-context models. [Evaluating Quantized Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18158) examines many families, quantizations and tasks, but **not this specific local paired Qwen setup**. These findings motivate, but do not replace, installed-hardware comparisons.

## Answer 3 — extraction, reasoning ensembles and correlated mistakes

Among completed relevance cases, **one** exact same-input, same-encoding, same-seed cross-model pair exists: both models returned the correct categorical answer 8. Thus the observed pair has agreement on a correct judgment. There are **zero paired wrong-wrong samples**, and the factual/logic task has **no matched cross-model pair yet**. It is impossible to estimate error correlation, ensemble gain or calibration from these data. Additional same/different-model seeds and equivalent prompt rewrites are required, with error dependence assessed **only against independent ground truth**. The fixed synthetic conditional, quotation, incomplete-ledger, and arithmetic oracles were specified before the model runs; a multiannotator/source-human validation layer remains outstanding for any subjectively labeled relevance corpus.

The offline oracle unit tests explicitly flag a two-model unanimous incorrect answer as false. This is a scorer test, **not evidence that the Qwen models committed that correlated error**. Primary [Self-Consistency](https://research.google/pubs/self-consistency-improves-chain-of-thought-reasoning-in-language-models/) reports improvements on selected reasoning benchmarks using sampled paths; agreement alone is not proof. The recent [agreement audit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08065) reports strong but regime-dependent overconfidence/failure correlations in sampled models, but is a research preprint with different benchmarks and **not a substitute for independently resolved local cases**.

## Answer 4 — syntactic/grammar auxiliary training

No model training, grammar fine-tuning or compute-matched local baseline was run. Loading/retraining full installed Qwen services would violate the production workload/disk constraint. A prompt containing parsed syntax is *not* a training-level auxiliary ablation. The answer from published studies is **conditional and mixed**, not a universal yes or no:

- [Glavaš and Vulić, EACL 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.270/) find limited, inconsistent downstream semantic-language-understanding benefit from intermediate supervised parsing of LM-pretrained transformers.
- [Strubell et al., EMNLP 2018](https://aclanthology.org/D18-1548/) find gains in semantic-role labeling with a syntactically informed multi-task self-attention architecture; this is **not** evidence of a general contemporary decoder-only LLM pretraining benefit.
- [Hu et al., ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.478/) report improved linguistic generalization and more token-efficient learning on their **formal-language pre-pretraining** setting, including a one-billion-parameter LM. This is a positive controlled *related intervention*, not exactly supervised POS/dependency auxiliary labels on installed Qwen.

A local generalization claim needs matched tokenizer, architecture, data split, seed budget, number of optimizer updates, FLOPs, training tokens and parameter count; a clean plain-LM baseline and shuffled-label/compute-matched negative control; evaluation on untouched natural language as well as syntax-sensitive distribution shifts. Training data vintage and parser error rates are additional confounders. Nothing in this continuation meets those conditions; no grammar-specific training winner is asserted.
